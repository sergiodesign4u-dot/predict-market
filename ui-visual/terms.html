How resolution works

1. What this document is

This section sets out what the agreement covers and what it does not. It explains that using Yonder means accepting a set of rules about the account, the funds inside it, and the events a person backs, and that the rules are written to be readable before they are written to be exhaustive.

It would also name the other documents that sit beside this one and say which of them wins if two disagree. In the shipped version that ordering is the first thing a reader is told, because a person who does not know which document governs cannot know what they have agreed to.

2. Who we are, and who this covers

This section identifies the operator of the service, the jurisdiction it is registered in, and the contact address for a legal notice. It states the minimum age and says that an account is personal: one person, one account, no acting on behalf of anyone else.

It would also describe what happens to an account opened by someone the rules do not cover, and it would say so before the deposit section rather than after it, because the answer changes whether the deposit should have been made at all.

3. Your account

This section describes registration, the information asked for at sign-up, and what a person is responsible for keeping safe. It explains that Yonder asks for an account only when a bet is confirmed and not when the site is opened, which is a product decision as much as a legal one and is written here so the two do not drift apart.

It would set out how identity is verified when a fiat deposit requires it, which is the only point at which more than an email is asked for, and it would say what an unverified account can and cannot do rather than leaving the reader to discover the limit at the moment it stops them.

It would also cover closing an account: how a person asks, how long it takes, and what happens to open positions and to a remaining balance.

4. Backing an event

This section describes what a bet is in this product. A person backs YES or NO on a stated question at the odds shown at the moment of confirmation, and the stake is taken from the account balance at that moment.

It explains that odds move because other people are backing the same event, so the price seen when an event is opened is not a promise about the price at confirmation. Where the two differ by more than a stated amount, the confirmation is shown again with the new number before anything is taken.

It would name the minimum and any maximum stake, say whether a bet can be cancelled and within what window, and state plainly that a bet placed on a mistaken reading of the question is still a bet.

5. How an event resolves

This section describes the resolution rule, which is the single most important thing this document decides. Every event names its source before it opens: the public record, feed or authority that will be read to settle it, and the moment at which that reading is taken.

It explains what happens when the named source is unavailable, contradicts itself, or has not reported by the stated time, and it would give the fallback in the same words on every event rather than case by case.

It would set out the dispute window: how long after settlement a person may challenge a resolution, what evidence is considered, who decides, and where the decision is recorded. The record is public, because a resolution nobody can check is a resolution nobody can trust.

It would describe the two cases that are not a simple reading of a source: an event whose question turns out to be ambiguous once the world has moved, and an event that becomes impossible to settle at all. For the first it would say who decides the reading and against what the decision is measured. For the second it would say that every stake is returned in full, and it would say it in the same sentence, because a rule about returning money is the one a person will look for under stress and should not have to assemble from two places.

It would also cover a recurring event, where each cadence instance settles on its own schedule against the same named source, and state that a later instance is a separate bet rather than a continuation of an earlier one.

6. Your money

This section describes custody. Funds are held as USDC in a segregated balance, one to one, and are not lent, staked, invested or used as working capital at any point.

It explains the difference between an available balance and a balance held against an open position, and it says which of the two can be withdrawn at any moment.

It would describe deposits and withdrawals: the routes available, the confirmations required, the time each normally takes, and what happens when a transfer fails or arrives at the wrong address. It would also state what happens to funds if the service stops operating, which is the question a reader of this page most often arrives with and the one most commonly left unanswered.

It would name the chain the balance is held on, the address at which the pooled balance can be read, and how a person matches their own figure against it. A custody claim that cannot be checked is a marketing line, and the difference between the two is a paragraph of instructions.

7. Fees

This section states every charge the service makes and when it is applied. A fee is shown before a bet is confirmed and never after, which is a rule about the interface as much as about the pricing.

It would set out the commission on a settled position, any deposit or withdrawal cost passed through from a payment route, and whether a fee can change without notice. A fee schedule that can move silently is not a schedule, so the notice period would be named here rather than implied.

8. Where the service is available

This section lists the places the service is not offered and the reason each is excluded. A person opening an account from an excluded place is told at sign-up rather than at withdrawal.

It would describe what happens to an existing account when a person moves, and what happens to positions that are open at that moment. It would also state that using a proxy or a VPN to reach the service from an excluded place is a breach, and what the consequence is.

9. Content you post

This section covers comments, display names and anything else a person publishes on the service. It sets out what may not be posted and what happens to content that breaks the rule.

It would state who owns what a person writes, what licence the service needs in order to show it, and how long content survives the account that wrote it.

10. When an account can be suspended or closed

This section lists the grounds on which the service may restrict, suspend or close an account, and it lists them exhaustively rather than ending with a general clause that swallows the list.

It would describe what a person is told and when, what happens to an open position and to a balance during a restriction, and how a decision is appealed. It would also say what the service may not do: a suspension is not a way to avoid settling a position that has already resolved.

11. If something goes wrong

This section describes the limits of what the service is responsible for, including outages, delayed settlement, and errors in the odds shown. It explains what is corrected and what is not.

It would state how a pricing error is handled once bets have been placed against it, which is the case where the interests of the service and of a person are furthest apart and therefore the case that most needs to be written down in advance.

12. Changes to this document

This section explains how the terms change and when a change takes effect. A change is effective immediately for a person opening an account after it is published, and after a stated notice period for a person who already has one. Both dates are shown at the top of this page, and the previous version stays available.

It would also say what a person may do if they do not accept a change, and confirm that closing an account and withdrawing a balance is always one of the options.

13. Law and disputes

This section names the law that governs the agreement and the forum in which a dispute is heard. It would say whether arbitration is required, whether a class action is waived, and what a person may do before either becomes necessary.

It would also give the informal route first: an address, a person, and a period in which a reply is due, because most disputes end there and a document that opens with arbitration reads as one that expects to be argued with.

14. How to reach us about this document

This section gives the single address for a question about these terms, separate from product support, and states the period within which a reply is sent.

It would also give the postal address for a formal notice, because some notices are only valid on paper and a person should not have to look for that.

A question about this document goes to legal@yonder.example, which is not product support. A reply is sent within five working days.
